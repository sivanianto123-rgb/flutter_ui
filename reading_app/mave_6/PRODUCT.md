# Mave

**Mave** is a reading app for toddlers. Learning is structured as a playful adventure: kids move through themed zones on a map, practice sounds and words through games, and unlock the next stage as they improve.

Tagline: *Where learning feels like magic.*

---

## The motive

The goal of Mave is **not** “know what the word means.” The goal is: **learn to read the word anyway.**

Many kids get stuck because reading is taught as vocabulary first — if they don’t know the word, they freeze. Mave flips that. By the time a child reaches longer words, they already have the tools to **decode**: hear sounds, spot vowels, spot consonants, and split the word into chunks they can say out loud.

Meaning can come later. First, the child should be able to look at something like *establish* and work through it piece by piece — even if they’ve never heard the word before.

That’s why the path is built in this order, and why Word Zone asks kids to mark vowels, then consonants, then split.

---

## Why the zones are ordered this way

Each zone exists to unlock the next skill. Nothing is random.

### 1. Phonics — teach the sounds

Before letters are “A” and “B” on a page, they are **sounds** the child can hear and say. Phonics Zone builds that ear: spin a sound, listen, play with it (bubble pop), meet it again in a short story.

Without sound awareness, later steps feel like guessing at shapes. With it, letters start to mean something you can pronounce.

### 2. Vowel Zone — know the “glue” of every word

Every readable chunk of English needs a vowel sound. Kids learn A, E, I, O, U clearly — what they look like, what they sound like, and how to find them among other letters.

Vowels come before consonants on purpose: when a child later looks at a word, the first useful move is *find the vowels*. That anchors where chunks can live.

### 3. Consonant Zone — know the other building blocks

Once vowels are solid, consonants get their turn: sorting them apart from vowels, hearing starting sounds, tying letters to simple words and pictures.

Now the child has both halves of the alphabet toolkit — not as a list to memorize, but as two families they can tell apart by feel.

### 4. Word Zone — put it together and decode

This is the heart of the method.

The child is asked to:

1. **Mark the vowels first** — find every vowel in the word.  
2. **Mark the consonants** — find the rest.  
3. **Split when two consonants sit together** — anytime the kid sees two consonants next to each other between vowel sounds, they split between them.

Example: **establish**

- Vowels give the skeleton of the word.  
- Between vowel chunks, consonants appear in pairs.  
- Split those pairs → **es / tab / lish**.

The child doesn’t need to “know” *establish*. They only need the habits from previous zones: sounds, vowels, consonants. Those habits turn a long word into short, sayable pieces.

That’s the whole point of Word Zone: **reading as decoding**, not as recalling vocabulary.

### 5. Sentence Zone — read in a line

Once words can be sounded out, sentences ask the child to follow words in order, line them up, and fill a missing word with a picture clue. Still playful — but now the pieces move left to right the way real reading does.

## How a session works

1. **Set up** — Enter the child’s name and a parent email.  
2. **Explore the map** — Scroll across islands; each one is a learning zone.  
3. **Play a zone** — Short games and activities teach that skill.  
4. **Unlock the next** — Doing well opens the following island.  
5. **Check progress** — Skills and overall progress are visible on the profile; parents can get a report.

**Unlock flow:** Phonics → Vowel → Consonant → Word → Sentence.

## What’s still early

- Content is still small (few phonics sounds; limited word and sentence sets)  
- Stories are simple templates, not rich personalized stories yet  
- Parent sign-in isn’t part of the main setup flow yet  
- Progress lives on the device only — not synced across phones yet  
- Background music and polish vary by platform  

---

## Design feel

Bright, cartoonish, and kid-first: forest scenes, playful buttons, progress bars with checkpoints, and short animated moments. The app runs full-screen so the world stays immersive.
